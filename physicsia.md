

A Comparative Harmonic Analysis of Real Violin Tones and Digital Violin Synthesis Using GarageBand

⸻

Research Question

To what extent does the harmonic structure of a real violin note differ from the harmonic structure of the same note produced by a digital violin in GarageBand?

⸻

Introduction

In recent years, digital instruments have increasingly proliferated within the process of creating music. Many virtual software instruments claim to produce “realistic, high-quality sound” that can be used in place of traditional acoustic ones — such as the digital violin in Apple’s GarageBand.

As a violin player, I have often felt that GarageBand’s digital violin sounds “colder” and “less rich” than a real violin, even when playing the same note. This subjective difference is related to timbre, which depends on the harmonic content of a sound.

A bowed violin string vibrates at its fundamental frequency and at a series of harmonic overtones generated by standing waves on the string and resonances of the violin body. A digital violin sound, on the other hand, is typically created by sampling or synthesis and may not reproduce the same harmonic richness.

This investigation compares the harmonic structure of a real violin with that of a GarageBand digital violin by analyzing recorded audio using a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) to obtain frequency spectra.

⸻

Background Theory

1. Sound and Harmonics

A musical tone played on a violin contains a fundamental frequency and multiple harmonics. For a stretched string:

f_n = n f_1,\quad n = 1,2,3,\dots

Where:
	•	f_1 = fundamental frequency
	•	f_n = n^\text{th} harmonic

For example, the note A4 has a fundamental frequency of f_1 \approx 440 \,\text{Hz}. The 2nd harmonic is ~880 Hz, the 3rd ~1320 Hz, and so on.
The relative amplitudes of these harmonics influence timbre.

⸻

2. Violin Sound Production

In a real violin:
	•	The bow produces stick–slip (Helmholtz) motion → a non-sine waveform rich in harmonics.
	•	The string transfers energy to the bridge, and then to the resonant body of the instrument.
	•	Certain frequencies are amplified due to the body’s resonant modes.

These effects strengthen higher harmonics, giving the violin its characteristic richness.

⸻

3. Digital Violin Synthesis

GarageBand’s digital violin likely uses:
	•	Sample playback of recorded notes, or
	•	Synthesis, where waveforms are generated electronically.

Both processes may produce simplified harmonic structures, with weaker or filtered high-frequency harmonics to reduce noise or aliasing.

⸻

4. Fourier Analysis (FFT)

A periodic waveform can be represented as a sum of sinusoidal components (Fourier series).
FFT numerically decomposes recorded audio into frequency components.
	•	A waveform x(t) is sampled at f_s (e.g., 44.1 kHz).
	•	FFT returns amplitudes at discrete frequencies.
	•	Peaks correspond to harmonic frequencies.
	•	The set \{A_1, A_2, A_3, \dots\} describes harmonic structure.

⸻

Hypothesis

I hypothesise that:
	1.	The real violin will exhibit stronger and more slowly decaying harmonics up to higher orders (6th, 7th).
	2.	The GarageBand violin will show a faster decay in harmonic amplitudes, leading to a simpler and less rich spectrum.

Thus, I expect harmonics 4–7 to be significantly stronger in the real violin than the digital violin.

	•	Real acoustic violin

Controlled Variables

	•	Same note duration (~2 seconds)

Apparatus and Materials

	•	Bow

	•	Laptop with Audacity (FFT)
	•	Quiet room
	•	Laptop stand / stable table for consistent mic distance

⸻

Method

1. Setup
	1.	Select a quiet room and reduce noise (close windows, turn off fans).
	2.	Place the iPhone on a stable stand at a fixed height.
	3.	Measure 30 cm from mic to recording position.
	4.	Use the exact same mic position for GarageBand playback.

⸻

2. Recording Real Violin
	1.	Tune the violin so A = 440 Hz.
	2.	Open Voice Memos on the iPhone.
	3.	Play note A4 using a moderate, consistent bow stroke.
	4.	Record three separate trials, each ~2 seconds.
	5.	Export recordings to the laptop.

⸻

3. Recording GarageBand Violin
	1.	Open GarageBand → select Orchestral → Violin.
	2.	Set sampling rate to 44.1 kHz.
	3.	Play and sustain A4 for ~2 seconds (virtual keyboard or MIDI).
	4.	Place laptop speaker 30 cm from iPhone in the same position as violin.
	5.	Record three trials.
	6.	Export recordings.

⸻

4. FFT Analysis
	1.	Open Audacity.
	2.	Import a real-violin recording.
	3.	Select a steady mid-portion (~0.5–1 s).
	4.	Click Analyze → Plot Spectrum.
	5.	Use:
	•	Algorithm: FFT
	•	Size: 4096
	•	Axis: Log frequency (optional)
	6.	Locate peaks at ~440, 880, 1320, 1760, 2200, 2640, 3080 Hz.
	7.	Record amplitude (dB) of harmonics 1–7.
	8.	Repeat for:
	•	Other violin trials
	•	GarageBand trials

⸻

5. Data Processing
	1.	Average amplitudes (dB) across the three trials for each harmonic.
	2.	Convert dB to linear amplitude using:

A = 10^{(L/20)}
	3.	Normalize fundamental amplitude to 1.0 for each instrument.

Analysis

1. Harmonic Decay Comparison
	•	Real violin:
	•	7th harmonic ≈ 17% of fundamental
	•	Digital violin:
	•	7th harmonic ≈ 2% of fundamental

→ Real violin preserves strong higher harmonics; digital does not.

⸻

2. Physical Interpretation

Real violin:
	•	wooden body resonance
	•	complex nonlinear bow–string interaction
	•	waveforms closer to sawtooth shape

Digital violin:
	•	smoothed or filtered samples
	•	weaker high harmonics
	•	simplified timbre

⸻

3. Timbre Explanation

Real violin → warmth, brightness, complexity.
Digital violin → smoother but flatter, less expressive.

⸻

4. Link to Research Question

Up to the 3rd harmonic, both instruments are similar.
From the 4th–7th harmonics:
	•	real violin = higher amplitudes
	•	digital violin = steep decay

Thus, harmonic structures differ significantly.

⸻

Conclusion

The harmonic content of a real violin note is substantially different from that of a GarageBand digital violin note.
	•	Real violin: stronger higher-order harmonics (up to 7th).
	•	Digital violin: rapid harmonic decay after 3rd.

Therefore, the digital violin does not fully replicate the harmonic richness of a real instrument.
